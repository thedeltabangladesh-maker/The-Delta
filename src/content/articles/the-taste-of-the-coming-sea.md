You can hear about sea-level rise in a hundred lectures and never quite feel it. In the coastal belt below Khulna, you feel it in the rice.

The grain grows shorter. The yield thins. The water from the tube well, once sweet, develops a faint mineral edge that no amount of boiling removes. Long before any tide gauge confirms it, families in the south-west know the sea is moving inland, because they can taste it.

## Salt does not knock

Salinity intrusion is one of the slower violences of a changing climate. It does not arrive in a single dramatic surge the way a cyclone does. It seeps. Reduced freshwater flow from upstream, combined with higher seas pushing in from the Bay of Bengal, lets saltwater travel further up the rivers and deeper into the soil each dry season.

The effect compounds. Salty soil grows less. Less harvest means less income. Less income means fewer ways to adapt, which leaves families even more exposed when the next dry season pushes the salt a little further still.

## Adapting, for now

People are not waiting to be rescued. Across the region, farmers have switched to salt-tolerant rice varieties where they can find them. Some have abandoned rice altogether for shrimp, flooding their fields with the very brackish water that ruined them for grain — a profitable move that brings its own slow damage to the land.

Others harvest rainwater through the wet months and ration it carefully through the dry, treating fresh water as the scarce resource it has quietly become.

These are clever, hard-won responses. But adaptation has a ceiling. There is a level of salt above which no tolerant seed will grow and no household budget will stretch. The question hanging over the coast is not whether people can adapt — they have proven, repeatedly, that they can — but for how long, and at what cost to the lives they would otherwise have chosen.

## A national question, posed locally

What happens on the southern coast does not stay there. As farmland turns saline and livelihoods fail, families move, often to Dhaka, joining a slow migration that reshapes the whole country.

The coast, in that sense, is an early-warning system for everyone. The taste of salt in a Khulna well is not only a local inconvenience. It is one of the clearest signals the delta has of where the water is heading, and how much time is left to decide what to do about it.
