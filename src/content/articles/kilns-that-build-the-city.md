In the dry months, the land around Dhaka sprouts chimneys. They rise from the flat fields in their hundreds, each one trailing a smudge of smoke, each one feeding the same hunger: the city's appetite for brick.

Bangladesh builds with brick because it has little stone and a great deal of clay. The kilns that turn one into the other are, in a real sense, the foundation of the modern country. They are also among the dirtiest single sources of the air that settles over the capital each winter, thick enough to taste.

## A seasonal economy of smoke

The traditional kiln is a brutally simple machine. Stack the green bricks, burn cheap fuel — often low-grade coal, sometimes wood or worse — and let the heat do its work over weeks. It is labour-intensive, fuel-hungry and almost entirely unregulated in practice, whatever the rules say on paper.

The human cost is twofold. There is the air, which carries fine particulates deep into the lungs of millions who never go near a kiln. And there is the workforce — often seasonal, often migrant, frequently including children — who live beside the fires for the length of the burning season.

## Cleaner ways to bake a brick

The technology to do better is not exotic. Improved kiln designs burn far more efficiently, using less fuel for the same output and releasing a fraction of the pollution. Some replace the fire-the-clay model entirely, pressing blocks from sand and cement, or from the fly ash that coal plants produce as waste.

Each alternative works in a laboratory and on a spreadsheet. The difficulty is never the engineering. It is the economics of an industry built on thin margins and cheap labour, where the cleaner option usually costs more up front and the dirty one carries no price for the harm it does to everyone downwind.

## Who pays for clean air

That is the policy knot at the centre of the problem. As long as a kiln can pour smoke into shared air at no cost to itself, the cleanest operator is simply the one with the highest bills.

Shifting that balance — through enforced standards, through incentives for better technology, through a construction sector willing to pay a little more for brick that does not poison the city — is less a technical project than a political one. The chimneys on the floodplain are not going away. The question is what comes out of them, and who decides.
