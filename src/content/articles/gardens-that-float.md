When the water refuses to drain, you have two choices. You can wait for land that may not return for months, or you can stop waiting for land altogether.

In the low-lying districts of the south, where monsoon flooding can leave fields submerged for half the year, some farmers have chosen the second path. They build their farms on the water itself — rafts of woven aquatic weed, packed with compost, riding the surface like green barges and growing vegetables where there is no longer any ground to speak of.

## An idea older than the crisis

The technique is not new. Versions of floating cultivation have existed in the region for generations, a quiet local answer to a landscape that has always spent part of the year underwater. What is new is the urgency, and the attention.

As flooding grows longer and less predictable, the floating garden has stopped looking like a curiosity and started looking like a strategy. Researchers who once filed it under folklore now study it as a model of how to keep food growing on a delta that is increasingly wet.

The rafts are built from water hyacinth, an invasive weed that clogs the waterways and that almost everyone otherwise treats as a nuisance. Layered and left to partly rot, it becomes a buoyant, nutrient-rich bed. The farmer plants directly into it. When the floods finally recede, the decomposed raft can be dug into the field as fertiliser. Almost nothing is wasted.

## The limits of a good thing

It would be easy to romanticise this, and many accounts do. A floating garden is genuinely elegant — cheap to build, made from waste, productive in conditions where ordinary farming fails.

But it is not a substitute for solid ground, and the families who rely on it would be the first to say so. The rafts are labour-intensive to build and maintain. They suit some crops and not others. They feed a household and supply a local market; they do not replace the staple-grain agriculture that feeds a nation.

What they offer is something more modest and, in its way, more important: a means of staying put. A floating garden lets a family ride out the flood season where they are, rather than joining the drift toward the cities when the water rises.

## A template, not a miracle

That is the real lesson of the floating gardens, and it applies far beyond the rafts themselves. The most durable climate adaptations in the delta tend to be the local ones — the practices that grew out of the place, that use what is already there, and that ask people to bend with the water rather than wall it out.

They will not solve the larger crisis. No single practice can. But they buy time, and dignity, and the chance to decide one's own future rather than have it decided by the next flood. On a changing delta, that is not a small thing to grow.
