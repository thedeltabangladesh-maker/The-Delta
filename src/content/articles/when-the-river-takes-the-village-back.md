The first thing to understand about river erosion in the Jamuna basin is that nobody is surprised by it. Surprise is a luxury for people who expect the ground to stay where they left it.

Along the braided channels north of Sirajganj, the river rewrites its own map every monsoon. A sandbar that carried three families and a mosque in March can be open water by August. The land does not so much disappear as relocate, surfacing again a kilometre downstream as a fresh char, raw and untested, waiting to be claimed.

## A calendar built around loss

Households here keep two kinds of time. There is the ordinary calendar of planting and harvest, and there is the river's calendar, which everyone reads from the colour and speed of the water. When the current turns heavy and brown, the older men start counting.

Families that have been displaced before know the drill. Tin roofing comes off first, because it is the most valuable and the easiest to carry. Then the wooden posts, the bedframes, the hand pump if there is time. What stays behind is whatever the water can have: the mud walls, the yard, the graves.

"You learn not to plant a tree you want to see grow old," one farmer told me, only half joking. He has moved four times in two decades, each time a little closer to the embankment road, each time with a little less.

## The embankment problem

For years the official answer to erosion has been the embankment — long earthen walls meant to hold the river to a fixed line. They work, in the way that a clenched fist works. The water that cannot spread sideways simply pushes harder somewhere else. Protect one bank and you often hand the problem to the village across the channel.

This is the uncomfortable arithmetic of hard engineering on a delta that was built, grain by grain, from sediment that is supposed to move. The Jamuna is not misbehaving when it shifts. It is doing the one thing a river of this size has always done.

## Living with the water, not against it

There is a quieter approach gaining ground among the people who actually live on the chars, and among some of the researchers who study them. Instead of trying to fix the land in place, it accepts that the land moves and tries to make the moving survivable.

That means portable housing designed to be dismantled in a day. It means land records flexible enough to follow a family when their plot dissolves. It means early-warning systems that read the river the way the old men do, but earlier and over a wider area.

None of this is glamorous. None of it makes a ribbon-cutting photograph. But on a delta where the ground itself is negotiable, the most radical idea may be the simplest one: stop fighting the river for territory it will reclaim anyway, and start planning for the day it does.
